How It Works & Mathematical Principles

A Solar Panel Savings Calculator sizes a residential rooftop photovoltaic system in kW DC and estimates 400W panel count, roof footprint, and annual electric bill savings from monthly kWh consumption, peak sun hours, and local utility rates.

Solar arrays rarely hit laboratory DC nameplate ratings 100% of the time because of inverter conversion, wiring resistance, temperature derating, and roof shading (~0.80 real-world derate factor). Accounting for actual Peak Sun Hours and derating gives realistic array sizing rather than optimistic hardware claims.

Governing Equation
System Size (kW) = (Monthly kWh × 12) / (Peak Sun Hours × 365 × 0.80)

Step-by-Step Worked Calculation Example

Scenario: Sizing a 100%-offset solar array for a home using 950 kWh/month with 4.5 peak sun hours/day and an $0.18/kWh utility tariff.

  • Annual yield per 1 kW DC (0.80 derate): 4.5 × 365 × 0.80 = 1,314 kWh/yr per kW.
  • Required DC array size: 11,400 / 1,314 = 8.6758 -> 8.68 kW DC.
  • 400W panels needed: ceil(8,680 / 400) = 22 panels (~462 sq ft roof area); est. gross annual bill offset = 11,400 × $0.18 = $2,052/yr.

Result: 8.68 kW DC Array (22 × 400W Panels; ~$2,052/yr Gross Savings)

Checking roof south/west unshaded area (~462 sq ft) early confirms whether 22 panels fit cleanly without shading losses.

Dividing Annual Usage by Total Daylight Hours Instead of Peak Sun Hours

12 hours of dim angled sunlight delivers far less irradiance than 12 peak 1,000 W/m² hours; always use regional Peak Sun Hours.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does "Peak Sun Hours" mean in solar sizing?

Peak sun hours represent equivalent hours per day at full 1,000 W/m² noon irradiance—not total daylight hours. Most US locations average 3.5 to 5.5 peak sun hours per day annually.

Why does the solar generation equation include a 0.80 efficiency factor?

Real photovoltaic systems lose roughly 15%–20% of nameplate DC output to inverter conversion, panel heat derating, wiring resistance, dust, and seasonal shading.

How does net metering improve residential solar payback?

Net metering credits surplus midday solar kilowatt-hours sent back to the utility grid against evening electricity consumption, cutting payback time substantially.
