How It Works & Mathematical Principles

The Bayes’ Theorem & Diagnostic Probability Calculator computes exact Posterior Probability P(A|B) (Positive Predictive Value), Negative Predictive Value (NPV), False Positive Rate, and Positive/Negative Likelihood Ratios (LR+, LR−) from base prevalence, sensitivity, and specificity.

Human intuition notoriously commits the "base-rate fallacy": if a screening test is 95% accurate (95% sensitivity, 92% specificity), most people assume a positive result means a ~95% chance the condition is present. In reality, when the underlying condition is rare (say 2% prevalence), false positives drawn from the 98% healthy majority outnumber true positives 4-to-1—leaving the true posterior probability at just 19.51%.

Governing Equation
P(A|B) = [P(B|A)·P(A)] ÷ [P(B|A)·P(A) + P(B|¬A)·(1 − P(A))]

Step-by-Step Worked Calculation Example

Scenario: A screening assay with 95% sensitivity and 92% specificity (8% false-positive rate) is applied to a population with 2.0% baseline prevalence.

  • True Positives per 10,000 people: 200 affected × 95% = 190 True Positives (1.90%).
  • False Positives per 10,000 people: 9,800 unaffected × (1 − 92%) = 784 False Positives (7.84%).
  • Posterior Positive Predictive Value: 190 ÷ (190 + 784) = 19.51% (NPV = 99.89%; LR+ = 11.88, LR− = 0.054).

Result: 19.51% Posterior Probability (PPV) · 99.89% NPV (190 TP vs 784 FP per 10k)

Despite 95% sensitivity and 92% specificity, over 4 in 5 positive screening results are false alarms because only 2% of the population had the condition.

Confusing Sensitivity P(Positive | Disease) With Positive Predictive Value P(Disease | Positive)

P(B|A) and P(A|B) are transposed conditionals—confusing them ignores base prevalence and wildly overstates confidence on rare events.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can a 95%-sensitive test produce only a 19.5% true-positive probability?

When baseline prevalence is low (e.g., 2.0%), 98% of tested individuals do not have the condition. Even a small 8% false-positive rate across that huge healthy majority generates 784 false positives vs. 190 true positives per 10,000 tested.

What do Positive and Negative Likelihood Ratios (LR+ and LR−) measure?

LR+ = Sensitivity ÷ (1 − Specificity) quantifies how much a positive result increases disease odds (LR+ > 10 is strong evidence), while LR− = (1 − Sensitivity) ÷ Specificity measures how strongly a negative test rules it out.

How do you apply Bayes’ Theorem when running a second confirmatory test?

Take the Posterior Probability (PPV) from the first test—for example 19.51%—and enter it as the new Prior Prevalence P(A) for an independent second assay to find combined post-test certainty.
