How It Works & Mathematical Principles

The Decibel (dB), dBm & Sound Attenuation Calculator computes logarithmic Power Gain/Loss (10·log₁₀ P₂/P₁ + dBm), Voltage / Sound Pressure Amplitude Ratios (20·log₁₀ V₂/V₁), and Free-Field Acoustic Inverse-Square Distance Drop (−20·log₁₀ r₂/r₁).

Because human hearing and RF signal links span orders of magnitude—from microWatts to kilowatts, or faint leaves (20 dB SPL) to jet engines (130 dB SPL)—engineers compress multiplicative ratios onto the logarithmic decibel scale. Crucially, Power ratios multiply log₁₀ by 10 (doubling power ≈ +3.01 dB), whereas root-power field quantities like Voltage or Sound Pressure multiply log₁₀ by 20 (doubling amplitude ≈ +6.02 dB).

Power: 10·log₁₀(P₂/P₁) | Voltage/SPL: 20·log₁₀(V₂/V₁) | Distance: SPL₁ − 20·log₁₀(r₂/r₁)

Step-by-Step Worked Calculation Example

Scenario: Comparing a 0.001 W (1 mW) RF input signal amplified to 0.1 W (100 mW) output in Power Gain mode.

  • Compute Power Ratio: P₂ ÷ P₁ = 0.1 ÷ 0.001 = 100× linear gain.
  • Evaluate Logarithmic Gain: 10 × log₁₀(100) = 10 × 2 = +20.00 dB.
  • Compute Absolute dBm Level: 10 × log₁₀(0.1 × 1,000 mW) = +20.00 dBm (re 1 mW).

Result: +20.00 dB Power Gain (100x Ratio · +20.00 dBm)

Every +10 dB multiplies power by 10× (+20 dB = 100×, +30 dB = 1,000×); in acoustic Distance Drop mode, stepping from 2m to 20m drops free-field noise by −20.00 dB.

Adding Two 80 dB Sound Sources Together as "160 dB"

Decibels add logarithmically, not linearly! Two identical uncorrelated 80 dB machines double acoustic power (+3.01 dB), yielding 83.0 dB—not 160 dB.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I use 10·log₁₀ (Power) versus 20·log₁₀ (Voltage / Sound Pressure)?

Use 10·log₁₀ whenever comparing energy or power quantities (Watts, mW, acoustic intensity). Because power varies with the square of voltage or pressure (P ∝ V²), comparing RMS voltages or acoustic Pascals multiplies log₁₀ by 20.

What is the difference between dB and dBm?

Plain dB is a purely relative gain/loss ratio between any two levels, whereas dBm is an absolute power measurement referenced specifically to 1 milliwatt (0.001 W = 0 dBm; 0.1 W = +20 dBm; 1 W = +30 dBm).

How fast does outdoor sound drop with distance in Free-Field Distance mode?

Under the geometric inverse-square law (−20·log₁₀ r₂/r₁), doubling your distance from an outdoor point-source sound drops sound pressure level by exactly −6.02 dB (and 10× distance drops −20.00 dB).
