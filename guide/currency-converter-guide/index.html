Currency Converter Guide: How Exchange Rates Work

Whether you're traveling abroad or sending money internationally, understanding exchange rates can save you significant money. Here's what you need to know.

What Is an Exchange Rate?

An exchange rate is the price of one currency expressed in terms of another. When you see EUR/USD = 1.08, it means 1 Euro buys 1.08 US Dollars. Exchange rates fluctuate constantly — sometimes by fractions of a percent per day, sometimes dramatically during economic crises. Understanding how they work helps you get better rates when traveling, sending money abroad, or making international purchases.

How Exchange Rates Are Determined

In the modern floating exchange rate system (used by most major economies), rates are set by supply and demand in the foreign exchange (forex) market — the world's largest financial market, with over $7 trillion traded daily.

Factors that move exchange rates:

Interest rates: Higher interest rates attract foreign capital seeking better returns, increasing demand for that currency and pushing its value up. When the US Federal Reserve raises rates, the dollar typically strengthens.

Inflation: Countries with lower inflation tend to have stronger currencies. High inflation erodes purchasing power, making the currency less valuable.

Economic performance: Strong GDP growth, low unemployment, and trade surpluses signal a healthy economy, attracting investment and strengthening the currency.

Political stability: Political uncertainty or instability causes investors to flee to "safe haven" currencies like the US Dollar, Swiss Franc, or Japanese Yen.

Market sentiment: Speculation and trader psychology can move currencies short-term, sometimes disconnected from economic fundamentals.

The Bid-Ask Spread: Why You Never Get the Mid-Market Rate

The mid-market rate (also called the interbank rate) is the midpoint between the buy and sell prices that banks trade at. It's the rate you see on Google or XE.com. But when you exchange currency, you never get this rate — banks and exchange services add a spread.

Example: Mid-market EUR/USD = 1.0800

  • Bank buys EUR from you at: 1.0650 (they profit $0.015 per euro)
  • Bank sells EUR to you at: 1.0950 (they profit $0.015 per euro)

The spread is how currency exchangers make money. Airport kiosks and hotel exchanges typically have the worst spreads (3–8%). Banks are better (1–3%). Online services like Wise (formerly TransferWise) offer rates closest to mid-market (0.3–1%).

Best Ways to Exchange Currency

For travel:

  1. Wise or Revolut debit cards: Exchange at near mid-market rates. Best option for most travelers.
  2. ATMs abroad: Usually offer better rates than exchange kiosks. Use your bank's partner ATMs to avoid fees.
  3. Credit cards with no foreign transaction fees: Chase Sapphire, Capital One Venture, and others charge 0% on foreign transactions and use Visa/Mastercard's competitive rates.
  4. Avoid: Airport kiosks, hotel exchanges, and currency exchange booths in tourist areas.

For international transfers:

  1. Wise: 0.3–1% above mid-market. Best for most transfers.
  2. OFX or Remitly: Competitive for large transfers.
  3. Bank wire transfers: Convenient but expensive (typically $25–$45 fee + 2–3% spread).
  4. PayPal: Convenient but charges 3–4% above mid-market.

Major Currency Pairs and Their Characteristics

EUR/USD (Euro/US Dollar): Most traded pair. Moves on ECB and Fed policy decisions, European economic data, and US economic reports.

USD/JPY (Dollar/Yen): Sensitive to risk sentiment. Yen strengthens during global uncertainty (safe haven). Affected by Bank of Japan policy.

GBP/USD (Pound/Dollar): Volatile pair. Heavily influenced by UK economic data and political events (Brexit caused dramatic swings).

USD/CHF (Dollar/Swiss Franc): Swiss Franc is a safe haven currency. Strengthens during global crises.

AUD/USD (Australian Dollar/US Dollar): Commodity currency — moves with gold and iron ore prices. Sensitive to China's economic health.

Understanding Currency Risk for Businesses

If you run a business that invoices in foreign currencies or pays international suppliers, exchange rate fluctuations can significantly impact profitability. A US company that invoices European clients in euros faces currency risk — if the euro weakens 5% before payment, that's a 5% revenue reduction.

Hedging strategies include:

  • Forward contracts: Lock in today's rate for a future transaction
  • Currency options: Right (but not obligation) to exchange at a set rate
  • Natural hedging: Match revenues and expenses in the same currency

How to Read Currency Quotes

Currency pairs are always quoted as Base/Quote. EUR/USD = 1.08 means:

  • Base currency: EUR (1 unit)
  • Quote currency: USD (1.08 units)
  • 1 Euro = 1.08 US Dollars

To convert $500 to euros: 500 ÷ 1.08 = €462.96

To convert €500 to dollars: 500 × 1.08 = $540
