How to Calculate BMI: Complete Guide with Examples

What Is BMI and Why Does It Matter?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a numerical value derived from your height and weight. It is one of the most widely used screening tools for identifying whether a person falls into a healthy weight range. Healthcare providers, researchers, and public health organizations use BMI as a quick, inexpensive way to assess potential weight-related health risks.

While BMI is not a diagnostic tool, it provides a useful starting point for conversations about weight management and overall health. People with a high BMI may be at greater risk for conditions such as type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure, and certain cancers.

The BMI Formula

There are two versions of the formula depending on which measurement system you use.

Metric Formula

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²

For example, if you weigh 70 kg and are 1.75 m tall:

BMI = 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9

Imperial Formula

BMI = (weight in pounds × 703) ÷ height in inches²

For example, if you weigh 154 lbs and are 5 feet 9 inches (69 inches) tall:

BMI = (154 × 703) ÷ (69 × 69) = 108,262 ÷ 4,761 = 22.7

Step-by-Step Calculation Example

Let us walk through a complete example using the metric system:

  1. Measure your weight: 80 kg
  2. Measure your height: 1.80 m
  3. Square your height: 1.80 × 1.80 = 3.24
  4. Divide weight by squared height: 80 ÷ 3.24 = 24.7
  5. Result: BMI of 24.7 (Normal weight)

The Four BMI Categories

The World Health Organization defines four standard BMI categories:

  • Underweight: BMI below 18.5
  • Normal weight: BMI 18.5 to 24.9
  • Overweight: BMI 25.0 to 29.9
  • Obese: BMI 30.0 and above

Obesity is further divided into Class I (30-34.9), Class II (35-39.9), and Class III (40 and above), sometimes called severe or morbid obesity.

Limitations of BMI

BMI is a useful screening tool, but it has significant limitations that you should understand:

  • Muscle mass is ignored: Athletes and bodybuilders often have a high BMI despite having very low body fat. Muscle weighs more than fat, so muscular individuals may be classified as overweight or obese.
  • Age is not considered: Older adults tend to have more body fat than younger adults at the same BMI. Children and teenagers require age- and sex-specific BMI charts.
  • Ethnicity differences: Research shows that people of Asian descent may face health risks at lower BMI thresholds, while some studies suggest different risk profiles for people of African descent.
  • Fat distribution is ignored: Where fat is stored matters. Abdominal (visceral) fat is more dangerous than fat stored in the hips and thighs, but BMI does not distinguish between the two.
  • Sex differences: Women naturally carry more body fat than men at the same BMI.

When to Use BMI vs Other Measures

BMI works best as an initial screening tool for large populations. For a more complete picture of your health, consider combining BMI with:

  • Waist circumference: A waist over 40 inches (men) or 35 inches (women) indicates higher risk regardless of BMI.
  • Waist-to-hip ratio: Helps identify abdominal obesity.
  • Body fat percentage: Measured via DEXA scan, hydrostatic weighing, or skinfold calipers for a more accurate assessment.
  • Blood tests: Cholesterol, blood sugar, and blood pressure provide direct health risk indicators.

Practical Tips for Maintaining a Healthy BMI

  • Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week
  • Incorporate strength training to build muscle and boost metabolism
  • Focus on whole foods, vegetables, lean proteins, and healthy fats
  • Monitor portion sizes rather than eliminating food groups
  • Get adequate sleep — poor sleep is linked to weight gain
  • Manage stress, which can trigger emotional eating
  • Track progress with regular weigh-ins, but do not obsess over daily fluctuations

Remember, BMI is just one data point. Work with your healthcare provider to interpret your BMI in the context of your overall health, lifestyle, and medical history.
