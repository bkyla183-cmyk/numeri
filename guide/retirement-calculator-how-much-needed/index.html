Retirement Calculator: How Much Do You Need to Retire?

The most common retirement question is: How much do I need? The answer depends on your lifestyle, timeline, and investment returns. Here's how to calculate it.

The Retirement Number Everyone Needs to Know

How much money do you need to retire comfortably? Financial planners have debated this for decades, but the most widely used answer comes from the 4% Rule: you need 25 times your annual expenses saved. If you spend $60,000 per year, you need $1.5 million. If you spend $40,000, you need $1 million. This rule is based on historical research showing that a portfolio of 60% stocks and 40% bonds can sustain 4% annual withdrawals for 30+ years without running out of money.

Why the 4% Rule Is a Starting Point, Not a Guarantee

The 4% rule was developed by financial planner William Bengen in 1994, based on US market data from 1926–1992. It's held up reasonably well, but it has limitations:

  • It assumes a 30-year retirement. If you retire at 55, you might need 40+ years of income.
  • It's based on US market returns, which have been historically strong.
  • It doesn't account for major expenses like healthcare or long-term care.
  • Sequence of returns risk — a market crash early in retirement is far more damaging than one later.

Many financial planners now recommend a 3–3.5% withdrawal rate for early retirees or those wanting extra security.

How to Calculate Your Retirement Number

Step 1: Estimate annual retirement expenses.

Most people spend 70–80% of their pre-retirement income in retirement. If you earn $80,000 now, budget $56,000–$64,000/year in retirement. But be specific: housing, food, healthcare, travel, hobbies, and gifts all add up.

Step 2: Subtract guaranteed income.

Social Security, pensions, and rental income reduce how much your portfolio needs to cover. If Social Security pays $24,000/year and you need $60,000, your portfolio only needs to cover $36,000/year.

Step 3: Apply the 4% rule.

$36,000 ÷ 0.04 = $900,000 needed in your portfolio.

Step 4: Account for inflation.

A dollar today won't buy the same in 20 years. At 3% inflation, $60,000 today equals $108,000 in 20 years. Your retirement calculator should factor in inflation-adjusted returns.

The Power of Starting Early

The single most powerful factor in retirement savings is time. Consider two investors:

  • Early starter: Invests $5,000/year from age 25–35 (10 years, $50,000 total), then stops.
  • Late starter: Invests $5,000/year from age 35–65 (30 years, $150,000 total).

At 7% annual return, the early starter has $602,000 at 65. The late starter has $472,000 — despite investing 3x more money. This is the magic of compound interest.

Social Security: What to Expect

Social Security replaces about 40% of pre-retirement income for average earners. You can claim as early as 62 (reduced benefits) or as late as 70 (maximum benefits). Waiting from 62 to 70 increases your monthly benefit by about 76%. For a married couple, coordinating claiming strategies can add $100,000+ in lifetime benefits.

Check your estimated benefits at ssa.gov/myaccount.

Retirement Account Types and Tax Strategy

Traditional 401k/IRA: Contributions are pre-tax; withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. Best if you expect to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement.

Roth 401k/IRA: Contributions are after-tax; withdrawals are tax-free. Best if you expect higher taxes in retirement or want tax-free income flexibility.

The ideal strategy: Diversify across both types. Having both taxable and tax-free accounts gives you flexibility to manage your tax bracket in retirement.

Healthcare: The Retirement Wild Card

Fidelity estimates the average couple needs $315,000 for healthcare costs in retirement (not including long-term care). Medicare starts at 65, but if you retire at 60, you need 5 years of private insurance — potentially $20,000–$30,000/year. Long-term care insurance or a dedicated health savings account (HSA) can help manage this risk.

How Much Should You Save Each Month?

A simple rule: save 15% of your gross income for retirement, including any employer match. If you start late, you may need 20–25%. Use the retirement calculator above to find your specific target based on your age, current savings, expected return, and retirement age.

The Retirement Readiness Checkpoints

Fidelity's age-based savings benchmarks:

  • Age 30: 1× your salary saved
  • Age 40: 3× your salary
  • Age 50: 6× your salary
  • Age 60: 8× your salary
  • Age 67: 10× your salary

These are guidelines, not guarantees. Your actual number depends on your lifestyle, health, and retirement goals. Use the calculator to build your personalized plan.
