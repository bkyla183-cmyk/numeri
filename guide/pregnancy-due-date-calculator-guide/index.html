How Due Dates Are Calculated

Pregnancy due dates are calculated using Naegele's Rule, a formula developed by German obstetrician Franz Karl Naegele in the early 19th century. The rule is simple:

Due Date = First Day of Last Menstrual Period (LMP) + 280 days (40 weeks)

Alternatively: Take the first day of your LMP, subtract 3 months, and add 7 days.

Example: If your LMP was January 1:

  • Add 280 days: October 8
  • Or: January 1 - 3 months = October 1, + 7 days = October 8

This calculation assumes a regular 28-day menstrual cycle with ovulation on day 14. Women with longer or shorter cycles may have a different actual conception date, which is why ultrasound dating is often used to refine the estimate.

Why Only 5% of Babies Are Born on Their Due Date

The due date is an estimate, not a deadline. Research shows that only about 5% of babies are born on their exact due date. Most births occur within a two-week window around the due date:

  • About 80% of births occur between 37 and 42 weeks
  • About 50% of births occur within one week of the due date
  • Spontaneous labor before 37 weeks is considered preterm
  • Labor after 42 weeks is considered post-term

Your due date is best understood as the midpoint of a normal range, not a precise prediction.

The Three Trimesters Explained

First Trimester (Weeks 1-12)

The first trimester begins on the first day of your last period and ends at 12 weeks. During this time:

  • The fertilized egg implants in the uterine wall
  • All major organs begin to form
  • The embryo becomes a fetus by week 10
  • The heart begins beating around week 6
  • Common symptoms: morning sickness, fatigue, breast tenderness, frequent urination
  • Miscarriage risk is highest in this trimester (about 10-20% of known pregnancies)

Second Trimester (Weeks 13-27)

Often called the "honeymoon trimester" because many women feel better:

  • Morning sickness typically improves
  • Energy levels often increase
  • The baby begins to move (felt as "quickening" around weeks 16-20)
  • Anatomy scan ultrasound typically performed around week 20
  • Baby develops hearing, and can respond to sounds by week 25
  • Significant weight gain begins

Third Trimester (Weeks 28-40)

The final stretch:

  • Baby gains most of its weight
  • Lungs mature in preparation for breathing
  • Baby moves into head-down position (usually by week 36)
  • Braxton Hicks contractions may increase
  • Common symptoms: back pain, shortness of breath, frequent urination, difficulty sleeping
  • Prenatal visits become more frequent (every 2 weeks, then weekly)

Key Milestones by Trimester

  • Week 6: Heartbeat detectable by ultrasound
  • Week 10: Embryo becomes a fetus; all organs formed
  • Week 12: End of first trimester; miscarriage risk drops significantly
  • Week 16-20: First fetal movements felt
  • Week 20: Anatomy scan; sex can often be determined
  • Week 24: Viability threshold (survival possible with intensive care)
  • Week 28: Third trimester begins; baby opens eyes
  • Week 32: Baby practices breathing movements
  • Week 36: Baby considered "early term"
  • Week 37: Baby considered "full term"
  • Week 40: Due date
  • Week 42: Post-term; induction typically recommended

Ultrasound Dating vs LMP Dating

LMP dating assumes a regular 28-day cycle, which many women do not have. Ultrasound dating measures the baby's size and is more accurate, especially when:

  • Cycles are irregular
  • The LMP date is uncertain
  • There is a significant discrepancy between LMP and ultrasound dates

First-trimester ultrasounds (before 14 weeks) are the most accurate for dating, with a margin of error of about 5-7 days. Later ultrasounds have larger margins of error.

What "Full Term" Means

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) defines:

  • Early term: 37 weeks 0 days to 38 weeks 6 days
  • Full term: 39 weeks 0 days to 40 weeks 6 days
  • Late term: 41 weeks 0 days to 41 weeks 6 days
  • Post-term: 42 weeks 0 days and beyond

Babies born at 39-40 weeks have the best outcomes. Elective deliveries before 39 weeks are generally discouraged unless medically necessary.

The Importance of Prenatal Care

Regular prenatal care is one of the most important things you can do for a healthy pregnancy. Prenatal visits allow your provider to:

  • Monitor your baby's growth and development
  • Screen for gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and other complications
  • Provide guidance on nutrition, exercise, and lifestyle
  • Administer recommended vaccines (flu, Tdap)
  • Address your questions and concerns
