The 4% Rule: Your Retirement Foundation

The 4% rule is the most widely cited guideline for retirement planning. It states that you can withdraw 4% of your portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjust that amount for inflation each subsequent year, and your portfolio should last at least 30 years.

The rule originated from the Trinity Study (1998), which analyzed historical stock and bond returns and found that a 4% withdrawal rate had a very high probability of portfolio survival over 30-year periods.

How to Calculate Your Retirement Number

Your retirement number is the total portfolio value you need to retire comfortably:

Retirement Number = Annual Expenses x 25

This is simply the inverse of the 4% rule (1 / 0.04 = 25).

Examples:

  • Annual expenses of $40,000: Need $1,000,000
  • Annual expenses of $60,000: Need $1,500,000
  • Annual expenses of $80,000: Need $2,000,000
  • Annual expenses of $100,000: Need $2,500,000

Social Security as Part of the Equation

Social Security reduces how much you need to save. If your annual expenses are $60,000 and you expect $20,000 per year from Social Security, you only need to fund $40,000 per year from your portfolio:

Portfolio needed = $40,000 x 25 = $1,000,000

Average retired-worker Social Security benefits run around $1,950–$2,070 per month (~$24,000+ per year) depending on claiming age and earnings history, alongside 2026 IRS retirement contribution limits of $24,500 for 401(k) plans and $7,500 for IRAs. Your actual benefit depends on your earnings history and the age at which you claim (62 to 70).

The Dramatic Impact of Starting Age

Time is the most powerful variable in retirement planning. The difference between starting at 25 vs 35 vs 45 is enormous:

Assuming 7% average annual return, saving $500 per month:

  • Starting at 25 (40 years): approximately $1,310,000 at 65
  • Starting at 35 (30 years): approximately $567,000 at 65
  • Starting at 45 (20 years): approximately $218,000 at 65

Starting 10 years earlier more than doubles your outcome. Starting 20 years earlier produces 6 times more wealth. This is the power of compound interest over time.

Sequence of Returns Risk

One of the biggest threats to retirement security is sequence of returns risk — the danger of experiencing poor investment returns early in retirement. If your portfolio drops 30% in your first year of retirement and you are withdrawing 4%, you may deplete your portfolio much faster than historical averages suggest.

Strategies to manage sequence risk:

  • Keep 1-2 years of expenses in cash or short-term bonds
  • Reduce withdrawals during market downturns if possible
  • Consider a flexible withdrawal strategy (withdraw less when markets are down)
  • Maintain some equity exposure throughout retirement for long-term growth

Inflation's Impact on Retirement Savings

Inflation erodes purchasing power over time. At 3% annual inflation, prices double approximately every 24 years. A retirement income of $60,000 today will need to be $108,000 in 24 years to maintain the same purchasing power.

This is why your retirement portfolio must continue growing during retirement, not just preserve capital. A portfolio of 100% bonds may feel safe but will likely fail to keep pace with inflation over a 30-year retirement.

Retirement Account Types

Traditional 401k and IRA

  • Contributions are pre-tax (reduce your taxable income now)
  • Investments grow tax-deferred
  • Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income
  • Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) starting at age 73

Roth 401k and Roth IRA

  • Contributions are after-tax (no immediate tax benefit)
  • Investments grow tax-free
  • Qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free
  • No RMDs for Roth IRA (Roth 401k has RMDs unless rolled to Roth IRA)

Which Is Better?

Generally: Roth is better if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement. Traditional is better if you expect to be in a lower bracket. Many advisors recommend having both for tax diversification.
