How Pregnancy Due Dates Are Calculated

A pregnancy due date (Estimated Due Date, or EDD) is calculated as 280 days (40 weeks) from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP). This is called Naegele's Rule, developed by German obstetrician Franz Karl Naegele in the 19th century. Despite its age, it remains the standard method used by healthcare providers worldwide.

Naegele's Rule formula:

EDD = LMP + 280 days

Or equivalently: Add 7 days to LMP, subtract 3 months, add 1 year.

Example: LMP = January 15, 2025

  • Add 7 days: January 22
  • Subtract 3 months: October 22
  • Add 1 year: October 22, 2025

This gives an EDD of October 22, 2025.

Why 40 Weeks If Conception Is at Week 2?

This is a common source of confusion. The 40-week pregnancy count starts from the LMP, not from conception. Ovulation and conception typically occur around day 14 of a 28-day cycle — so you're already "2 weeks pregnant" at the moment of conception. The 40-week count includes these 2 weeks before conception.

Actual fetal development time from conception to birth is approximately 38 weeks.

The Three Trimesters Explained

First Trimester (Weeks 1–12)

The most critical period for fetal development. All major organs begin forming. Morning sickness, fatigue, and breast tenderness are common. Miscarriage risk is highest in this trimester (about 10–20% of known pregnancies). The first prenatal visit typically occurs at 8–10 weeks.

Key milestones:

  • Week 6: Heartbeat detectable by ultrasound
  • Week 8: All major organs present in rudimentary form
  • Week 10: Fetus is fully formed; now called a fetus (previously embryo)
  • Week 12: Risk of miscarriage drops significantly

Second Trimester (Weeks 13–26)

Often called the "honeymoon trimester" — morning sickness typically subsides, energy returns, and the pregnancy becomes visible. The fetus grows rapidly and begins moving.

Key milestones:

  • Week 16: Gender may be visible on ultrasound
  • Week 18–20: Anatomy scan (detailed ultrasound)
  • Week 20: Halfway point; fetus is about 10 inches long
  • Week 24: Viability threshold — premature birth survival becomes possible

Third Trimester (Weeks 27–40)

Rapid weight gain for both mother and baby. The baby's lungs mature, and it moves into head-down position. Braxton Hicks contractions (practice contractions) become common.

Key milestones:

  • Week 28: Third trimester begins; weekly prenatal visits start
  • Week 32: Baby's bones fully developed (still soft)
  • Week 36: Baby considered "early term"
  • Week 37–38: "Full term" — lungs fully mature
  • Week 40: Due date
  • Week 42: "Post-term" — induction typically recommended

How Accurate Is the Due Date?

Only about 5% of babies are born on their exact due date. Most births occur within 2 weeks before or after the EDD. The normal range for full-term birth is 37–42 weeks.

Factors that affect accuracy:

  • Irregular cycles: Naegele's Rule assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14. Longer or shorter cycles shift the actual conception date.
  • Ultrasound dating: A first-trimester ultrasound (before 14 weeks) is more accurate than LMP dating and may adjust the EDD.
  • IVF pregnancies: The exact fertilization date is known, making dating more precise.

Prenatal Care Schedule

Typical prenatal visit schedule for a low-risk pregnancy:

  • Weeks 4–28: Every 4 weeks
  • Weeks 28–36: Every 2 weeks
  • Weeks 36–40: Every week

Key tests by trimester:

  • First trimester: Blood type, CBC, STI screening, genetic carrier screening, nuchal translucency ultrasound (11–14 weeks)
  • Second trimester: Anatomy scan (18–20 weeks), glucose tolerance test (24–28 weeks), quad screen
  • Third trimester: Group B strep test (35–37 weeks), non-stress tests if needed

Signs of Labor

As the due date approaches, watch for:

  • Lightening: Baby drops lower into the pelvis (2–4 weeks before labor)
  • Bloody show: Mucus plug discharge
  • Water breaking: Rupture of membranes (only 10% of labors start this way)
  • Regular contractions: 5 minutes apart, lasting 60 seconds, for 1 hour (5-1-1 rule)
