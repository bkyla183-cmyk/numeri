The Compound Interest Formula Explained

Compound interest is one of the most powerful forces in personal finance. Understanding how to calculate it step by step gives you the ability to project investment growth, compare savings accounts, and understand the true cost of debt.

The formula is: A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

Where each variable means:

  • A = Final amount (what you end up with)
  • P = Principal (your starting amount)
  • r = Annual interest rate expressed as a decimal (5% = 0.05)
  • n = Number of times interest compounds per year
  • t = Time in years

5 Step-by-Step Examples

Example 1: Basic Annual Compounding

You invest $5,000 at 6% annual interest, compounded annually, for 5 years.

A = $5,000 x (1 + 0.06/1)^(1x5)

A = $5,000 x (1.06)^5

A = $5,000 x 1.3382

A = $6,691.13

Example 2: Monthly Compounding

Same $5,000 at 6% but compounded monthly for 5 years.

A = $5,000 x (1 + 0.06/12)^(12x5)

A = $5,000 x (1.005)^60

A = $5,000 x 1.3489

A = $6,744.25

Monthly compounding earns $53.12 more than annual compounding over 5 years.

Example 3: Daily Compounding

Same $5,000 at 6% compounded daily for 5 years.

A = $5,000 x (1 + 0.06/365)^(365x5)

A = $5,000 x (1.0001644)^1825

A = $5,000 x 1.3498

A = $6,749.29

Example 4: Long-Term Growth (30 Years)

$10,000 invested at 7% compounded monthly for 30 years.

A = $10,000 x (1 + 0.07/12)^(12x30)

A = $10,000 x (1.005833)^360

A = $10,000 x 8.1165

A = $81,165

Your $10,000 grows to over $81,000 — more than 8 times your original investment.

Example 5: Regular Contributions

If you add $200 per month to a $10,000 starting balance at 7% for 30 years, you would accumulate approximately $284,000 — demonstrating the power of combining compound interest with regular contributions.

Continuous Compounding Formula

When interest compounds continuously (the mathematical limit as n approaches infinity):

A = P x e^(rt)

Where e is Euler's number (approximately 2.71828).

Example: $1,000 at 5% continuously compounded for 10 years:

A = $1,000 x e^(0.05 x 10)

A = $1,000 x e^0.5

A = $1,000 x 1.6487

A = $1,648.72

Simple vs Compound Interest Comparison

Starting with $10,000 at 6% annual interest:

  • After 10 years: Simple = $16,000 | Compound (annual) = $17,908 | Difference = $1,908
  • After 20 years: Simple = $22,000 | Compound (annual) = $32,071 | Difference = $10,071
  • After 30 years: Simple = $28,000 | Compound (annual) = $57,435 | Difference = $29,435

The gap between simple and compound interest grows exponentially over time. This is why starting to invest early is so critical.

How Compounding Frequency Affects Growth

Using $10,000 at 8% for 20 years:

  • Annual compounding: $46,610
  • Quarterly compounding: $47,911
  • Monthly compounding: $48,454
  • Daily compounding: $48,675

The difference between annual and daily compounding is about $2,065 over 20 years on a $10,000 investment.

Practical Applications

Savings Accounts

High-yield savings accounts typically compound daily. When comparing accounts, look at the APY (Annual Percentage Yield), which already accounts for compounding frequency and gives you the true annual return.

Investing

Stock market returns compound over time when dividends are reinvested. A 7% average annual return compounded over 40 years turns $10,000 into approximately $149,745.

Debt

Credit card debt at 24% APR compounded daily means your balance grows by about 0.066% per day. A $5,000 balance with no payments becomes $6,394 after one year.

How to Use a Compound Interest Calculator Effectively

  • Always input the annual rate, not the monthly rate
  • Select the correct compounding frequency (check your account terms)
  • For investments with regular contributions, use the future value with payments formula
  • Compare APY across accounts rather than APR for an apples-to-apples comparison
  • Run scenarios with different time horizons to see the impact of starting earlier
