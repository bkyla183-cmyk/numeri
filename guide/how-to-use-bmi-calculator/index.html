What Is BMI and Why Is It Used?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a simple screening tool that estimates whether a person's weight is appropriate for their height. Developed by Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet in the 1830s, BMI has become the most widely used weight classification tool in medicine and public health — not because it's perfect, but because it's fast, free, and requires only two measurements.

BMI is used by doctors, insurance companies, researchers, and public health agencies to quickly categorize weight status across large populations. It's a starting point for health assessment, not a definitive diagnosis.

The BMI Formula

Metric: BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²)

Imperial: BMI = (weight (lbs) × 703) ÷ height² (inches²)

Example (metric): 70 kg, 175 cm tall

  • Height in meters: 175 ÷ 100 = 1.75 m
  • BMI = 70 ÷ (1.75)² = 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9

Example (imperial): 154 lbs, 5'9" (69 inches)

  • BMI = (154 × 703) ÷ (69)² = 108,262 ÷ 4,761 = 22.7

These categories apply to adults 20 and older. Children and teens use age- and sex-specific percentile charts.

What BMI Predicts (and What It Doesn't)

What BMI correlates with:

  • Type 2 diabetes risk
  • Cardiovascular disease risk
  • Hypertension
  • Sleep apnea
  • Certain cancers
  • Joint problems

What BMI cannot measure:

  • Body fat percentage
  • Muscle mass
  • Fat distribution (where fat is stored matters enormously)
  • Bone density
  • Metabolic health

A muscular athlete with 10% body fat might have a BMI of 28 ("overweight") because muscle is denser than fat. Conversely, a sedentary person with 35% body fat might have a "normal" BMI of 23. This is why BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis.

BMI Limitations by Population

Athletes and bodybuilders: Muscle mass inflates BMI. A 200-lb, 5'10" athlete with 8% body fat has a BMI of 28.7 (overweight) — clearly inaccurate.

Older adults: BMI underestimates health risk in the elderly. Older people lose muscle mass (sarcopenia) while gaining fat, so a "normal" BMI may mask unhealthy body composition.

Different ethnicities: Research shows that Asian populations have higher health risks at lower BMI values. Many Asian countries use lower cutoffs: overweight at BMI 23+, obese at 27.5+.

Women vs. men: Women naturally have higher body fat percentages than men at the same BMI. A BMI of 25 represents about 20% body fat in men but 30% in women.

Better Measures to Use Alongside BMI

Waist circumference: Abdominal fat (visceral fat) is more dangerous than fat elsewhere. Risk increases above 35 inches (women) or 40 inches (men).

Waist-to-height ratio: Waist circumference ÷ height. Values above 0.5 indicate increased health risk. Simple rule: your waist should be less than half your height.

Body fat percentage: Measured by DEXA scan (most accurate), hydrostatic weighing, or bioelectrical impedance. Healthy ranges: 10–20% for men, 18–28% for women.

Waist-to-hip ratio: Waist ÷ hip circumference. Above 0.90 (men) or 0.85 (women) indicates abdominal obesity.

Healthy Weight Range by Height

Using the 18.5–24.9 BMI range:

  • 5'4" (163 cm): 108–145 lbs (49–66 kg)
  • 5'6" (168 cm): 115–154 lbs (52–70 kg)
  • 5'8" (173 cm): 122–164 lbs (55–74 kg)
  • 5'10" (178 cm): 129–174 lbs (59–79 kg)
  • 6'0" (183 cm): 136–184 lbs (62–83 kg)
  • 6'2" (188 cm): 144–194 lbs (65–88 kg)

What to Do With Your BMI Result

If underweight (BMI < 18.5): Consult a doctor to rule out underlying conditions. Focus on nutrient-dense foods and strength training to build muscle mass.

If normal weight (18.5–24.9): Maintain through balanced diet and regular exercise. Monitor waist circumference as an additional health indicator.

If overweight (25–29.9): Even modest weight loss (5–10%) significantly reduces health risks. Focus on sustainable lifestyle changes rather than crash diets.

If obese (30+): Medical guidance is recommended. Evidence-based interventions include structured diet programs, increased physical activity, behavioral therapy, and in some cases medication or surgery.
