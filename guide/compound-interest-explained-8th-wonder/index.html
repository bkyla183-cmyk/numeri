What Is Compound Interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. In contrast, simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. This seemingly small difference has an enormous impact over time, which is why Albert Einstein allegedly called compound interest the eighth wonder of the world.

With simple interest, if you invest $1,000 at 5% annually, you earn $50 every year — always on the original $1,000. With compound interest, you earn interest on your interest, so your balance grows exponentially rather than linearly.

The Compound Interest Formula

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

Where:

  • A = the final amount (principal + interest)
  • P = the principal (initial investment)
  • r = annual interest rate (as a decimal, so 5% = 0.05)
  • n = number of times interest compounds per year
  • t = time in years

Worked Example: $1,000 at 5% for 10 Years

Let us compare simple vs compound interest on $1,000 at 5% annual interest over 10 years:

Simple interest:

A = 1,000 + (1,000 × 0.05 × 10) = $1,500

Compound interest (annual compounding):

A = 1,000 × (1 + 0.05/1)^(1×10) = 1,000 × 1.6289 = $1,628.89

The difference is $128.89 — and that gap widens dramatically over longer periods.

The Power of Compounding Frequency

How often interest compounds makes a significant difference. Using the same $1,000 at 5% for 10 years:

  • Annual compounding: $1,628.89
  • Monthly compounding: $1,647.01
  • Daily compounding: $1,648.66
  • Continuous compounding: $1,648.72

The more frequently interest compounds, the more you earn. Daily compounding earns about $20 more than annual compounding over 10 years — and that difference scales up with larger amounts and longer time horizons.

Real-World Applications

Savings and Investments

High-yield savings accounts, certificates of deposit (CDs), and investment accounts all use compound interest. The stock market historically returns around 7-10% annually, and reinvesting dividends compounds your returns significantly over decades.

Retirement Accounts

A 25-year-old who invests $5,000 per year at 7% will have approximately $1.1 million by age 65. A 35-year-old doing the same will have only about $540,000. Starting 10 years earlier more than doubles the outcome.

Debt

Compound interest works against you when you carry debt. Credit cards typically charge 20-30% APR, compounded daily. A $5,000 balance at 24% APR with minimum payments can take over 15 years to pay off and cost more than $6,000 in interest alone.

The Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 is a quick mental math shortcut to estimate how long it takes to double your money at a given interest rate:

Years to double = 72 ÷ interest rate

Examples:

  • At 6% interest: 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years to double
  • At 8% interest: 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years to double
  • At 12% interest: 72 ÷ 12 = 6 years to double

This rule also works in reverse for debt — a credit card at 24% APR will double your balance in just 3 years if you make no payments.

How to Use Compound Interest to Build Wealth

  • Start as early as possible: Time is the most powerful variable in the compound interest formula. Even small amounts invested early outperform large amounts invested late.
  • Reinvest all earnings: Do not withdraw interest or dividends — let them compound.
  • Increase contributions over time: As your income grows, increase your investment amounts.
  • Choose accounts with higher compounding frequency: Daily compounding beats monthly, which beats annual.
  • Minimize fees: A 1% annual fee on a $100,000 portfolio costs over $30,000 over 20 years due to the compounding effect of lost returns.
  • Be consistent: Regular contributions, even during market downturns, maximize the compounding effect.

Warning: Compound Interest Works Against You in Debt

The same force that builds wealth can destroy it. High-interest debt compounds just as relentlessly as investments. Always prioritize paying off high-interest debt before investing, and never carry a credit card balance if you can avoid it. The math that makes compound interest wonderful for savers makes it devastating for borrowers.
