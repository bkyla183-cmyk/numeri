Loan Amortization Explained: How Your Monthly Payment Is Calculated

Ever wonder why most of your early mortgage payments go to interest? Learn how loan amortization works and how to use it to your advantage.

What Does Amortization Mean?

Amortization is the process of paying off a debt through regular scheduled payments over time. Each payment covers both interest and principal, and the loan is fully paid off at the end of the term. The word comes from the Latin "amortire," meaning to kill off — you are gradually killing off the debt.

Unlike interest-only loans (where you only pay interest and the principal never decreases), amortizing loans are designed to be fully paid off by the end of the term. This is the standard structure for mortgages, auto loans, and personal loans.

Why Early Payments Are Mostly Interest

Here is the counterintuitive reality of amortization: in the early months of a loan, the vast majority of your payment goes to interest, not principal. This is because interest is calculated on the outstanding balance, which is highest at the beginning.

Example: Month 1 of a $300,000 mortgage at 6.5% for 30 years (monthly payment = $1,896):

  • Monthly interest rate = 6.5% / 12 = 0.5417%
  • Interest for month 1 = $300,000 x 0.005417 = $1,625
  • Principal for month 1 = $1,896 - $1,625 = $271

So in month 1, only $271 of your $1,896 payment reduces your loan balance. The remaining $1,625 is pure interest.

By month 180 (year 15), the split has shifted:

  • Remaining balance: approximately $218,000
  • Interest = $218,000 x 0.005417 = $1,181
  • Principal = $1,896 - $1,181 = $715

By month 360 (final payment):

  • Remaining balance: approximately $1,886
  • Interest = approximately 10
  • Principal = approximately $1,886

How to Read an Amortization Table

An amortization table (or schedule) shows every payment over the life of the loan with columns for:

  • Payment number
  • Payment amount (constant for fixed-rate loans)
  • Interest portion
  • Principal portion
  • Remaining balance

Reading the table reveals how slowly your balance decreases in the early years. After 5 years of payments on a 30-year mortgage, you have paid off less than 5% of the principal.

The Amortization Formula

The monthly payment formula is: M = P x [r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n - 1]

Where P is principal, r is monthly interest rate, and n is total number of payments.

Once you have the monthly payment, each month's interest is: Balance x Monthly Rate

And each month's principal is: Monthly Payment - Interest

Front-Loading of Interest Explained

The front-loading of interest is not a trick by lenders — it is a mathematical consequence of how interest works. You owe interest on whatever balance remains. Since the balance is highest at the start, interest is highest at the start.

This front-loading has an important implication: extra payments made early in the loan save dramatically more interest than the same extra payments made later. A $1,000 extra payment in month 1 eliminates $1,000 of principal that would have accrued interest for the next 29 years. The same $1,000 extra payment in year 25 only eliminates 5 years of interest.

How Extra Payments Dramatically Reduce Total Interest

On a $300,000 mortgage at 6.5% for 30 years:

  • Standard payments: Total interest = $382,560
  • Extra $100/month: Total interest = approximately $352,000 (saves $30,000, pays off 4 years early)
  • Extra $200/month: Total interest = approximately $330,000 (saves $52,000, pays off 6 years early)
  • Extra $500/month: Total interest = approximately $282,000 (saves $100,000, pays off 10 years early)

Amortizing vs Interest-Only Loans

  • Amortizing loan: Each payment reduces the principal. Balance decreases over time. Loan is fully paid off at end of term.
  • Interest-only loan: Payments cover only interest. Principal does not decrease. At the end of the interest-only period, you still owe the full original amount (or must make a balloon payment).

Interest-only loans have lower initial payments but are riskier because you build no equity and face a large payment at the end.

Balloon Payments

Some loans have a balloon payment — a large lump sum due at the end of the loan term. For example, a 5-year balloon mortgage might have payments calculated as if it were a 30-year loan, but the entire remaining balance is due after 5 years. These loans offer lower initial payments but require refinancing or a large payment at maturity.

How to Generate Your Own Amortization Schedule

You can create an amortization schedule in a spreadsheet:

  1. Calculate monthly payment using the formula above
  2. For each row: Interest = Previous Balance x Monthly Rate
  3. Principal = Monthly Payment - Interest
  4. New Balance = Previous Balance - Principal
  5. Repeat for each payment period

Most mortgage calculators and financial websites will generate a complete amortization schedule automatically when you input your loan details.
