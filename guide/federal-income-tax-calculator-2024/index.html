How Progressive Tax Brackets Work

One of the most misunderstood concepts in personal finance is how tax brackets work. Many people believe that earning more money can somehow result in taking home less money — this is a myth. The US federal income tax system is progressive, meaning higher rates apply only to income above each threshold, not to your entire income.

Think of tax brackets as buckets. The first bucket fills up at the lowest rate. Once it is full, income spills into the next bucket at a slightly higher rate. You never pay the higher rate on income that already filled a lower bucket.

2026 Federal Tax Brackets for Single Filers

  • 10%: On taxable income from $0 to $12,400
  • 12%: On taxable income from $12,401 to $50,400
  • 22%: On taxable income from $50,401 to $105,700
  • 24%: On taxable income from $105,701 to $201,775
  • 32%: On taxable income from $201,776 to $256,225
  • 35%: On taxable income from $256,226 to $640,600
  • 37%: On taxable income above $640,600

Worked Example: $75,000 Income

Let us calculate the federal income tax for a single filer with $75,000 in taxable income (after deductions):

  • 10% on first $12,400 = $1,240
  • 12% on next $38,000 ($12,401 to $50,400) = $4,560
  • 22% on remaining $24,600 ($50,401 to $75,000) = $5,412

Total federal tax = $1,240 + $4,560 + $5,412 = $11,212

Effective vs Marginal Tax Rate

  • Marginal tax rate: The rate applied to your last dollar of income. For our $75,000 example, the marginal rate is 22%.
  • Effective tax rate: Your total tax divided by your total income. For our example: $11,212 / $75,000 = 14.95%.

Your effective rate is always lower than your marginal rate. When people say "I'm in the 22% bracket," they do not mean they pay 22% on all their income — they mean their highest bracket is 22%.

The Standard Deduction

Before applying tax brackets, you subtract the standard deduction from your gross income to get taxable income:

  • Single filers: $16,100 (2026)
  • Married filing jointly: $32,200 (2026)
  • Head of household: $24,150 (2026)

Example: If you earn $75,000 and take the standard deduction as a single filer:

Taxable income = $75,000 - $16,100 = $58,900 ($7,670 federal tax)

Your tax would be calculated on $58,900 ($7,670 federal tax), not $75,000.

Common Deductions and Credits

Deductions (reduce taxable income)

  • Mortgage interest (if you itemize)
  • State and local taxes (SALT) — capped at $10,000
  • Charitable contributions
  • Student loan interest (up to $2,500)
  • Health savings account (HSA) contributions
  • Traditional IRA contributions (income limits apply)

Credits (directly reduce tax owed — more valuable than deductions)

  • Child Tax Credit: up to $2,000 per qualifying child
  • Earned Income Tax Credit: up to $7,830 for families with 3+ children
  • Child and Dependent Care Credit
  • American Opportunity Tax Credit: up to $2,500 for college expenses
  • Retirement Savings Contributions Credit (Saver's Credit)

W-4 Withholding

Your employer withholds federal income tax from each paycheck based on your W-4 form. If too much is withheld, you get a refund. If too little is withheld, you owe money at tax time. Update your W-4 after major life changes (marriage, divorce, new child, second job).

Estimated Taxes for Self-Employed

If you are self-employed, freelance, or have significant investment income, you must pay estimated taxes quarterly (due in April, June, September, and January). The self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) adds 15.3% on top of income tax for self-employed individuals, though half is deductible.
