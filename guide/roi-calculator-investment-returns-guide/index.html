What Is ROI?

Return on Investment (ROI) is one of the most widely used metrics in business and personal finance. It measures the efficiency of an investment by expressing the gain or loss relative to the cost of the investment as a percentage.

ROI answers the fundamental question: "For every dollar I put in, how much did I get back?"

The ROI Formula

ROI = (Net Profit / Cost of Investment) x 100

Where:

  • Net Profit = Final Value - Cost of Investment
  • Cost of Investment = the total amount invested (including fees, taxes, and other costs)

Worked Examples

Example 1: Stock Investment

You buy 100 shares of a stock at $50 per share (total investment: $5,000). One year later, the stock is worth $65 per share (total value: $6,500). You also received $200 in dividends.

Net Profit = ($6,500 + $200) - $5,000 = $1,700

ROI = ($1,700 / $5,000) x 100 = 34%

Example 2: Real Estate

You buy a rental property for $200,000 (including closing costs). After one year, you have collected $18,000 in rent and paid $8,000 in expenses (mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, maintenance). The property is now worth $215,000.

Net Profit = ($215,000 - $200,000) + ($18,000 - $8,000) = $15,000 + $10,000 = $25,000

ROI = ($25,000 / $200,000) x 100 = 12.5%

Example 3: Business Investment

You invest $10,000 in new equipment for your business. Over the next year, the equipment generates $15,000 in additional revenue with $3,000 in operating costs.

Net Profit = $15,000 - $3,000 - $10,000 = $2,000

ROI = ($2,000 / $10,000) x 100 = 20%

Example 4: Marketing Campaign

You spend $2,000 on a digital marketing campaign. The campaign generates $8,500 in new sales with a 40% profit margin, so profit from new sales = $3,400.

Net Profit = $3,400 - $2,000 = $1,400

ROI = ($1,400 / $2,000) x 100 = 70%

Annualized ROI for Multi-Year Investments

Simple ROI does not account for time. A 50% ROI over 10 years is very different from a 50% ROI over 1 year. The annualized ROI formula accounts for this:

Annualized ROI = ((1 + ROI)^(1/n) - 1) x 100

Where n = number of years.

Example: A 50% total ROI over 5 years:

Annualized ROI = ((1 + 0.50)^(1/5) - 1) x 100

Annualized ROI = (1.50^0.2 - 1) x 100

Annualized ROI = (1.0845 - 1) x 100 = 8.45% per year

ROI vs Other Investment Metrics

IRR (Internal Rate of Return)

IRR is the discount rate that makes the net present value of all cash flows equal to zero. It accounts for the timing of cash flows, making it more accurate than simple ROI for complex investments with multiple cash flows over time.

NPV (Net Present Value)

NPV calculates the present value of all future cash flows minus the initial investment. A positive NPV means the investment creates value; negative NPV destroys value.

Payback Period

The payback period is how long it takes to recover your initial investment. Simple to calculate but ignores cash flows after the payback period and the time value of money.

Limitations of ROI

  • Does not account for time value of money: A 20% ROI over 1 year is much better than 20% over 10 years, but simple ROI treats them the same.
  • Does not account for risk: Two investments with the same ROI may have very different risk profiles.
  • Can be manipulated: By choosing what to include or exclude from "costs," ROI can be made to look better or worse.
  • Does not capture opportunity cost: A 10% ROI might be excellent or terrible depending on what else you could have done with the money.

Comparing Investments With Different Time Horizons

Always use annualized ROI when comparing investments that span different time periods. A 100% ROI over 10 years (7.18% annualized) is worse than a 50% ROI over 5 years (8.45% annualized).

Negative ROI and What It Means

A negative ROI means you lost money on the investment. Net Profit is negative when the final value is less than the cost.

Example: You invest $10,000 in a startup. The company fails and you recover $2,000.

Net Profit = $2,000 - $10,000 = -$8,000

ROI = (-$8,000 / $10,000) x 100 = -80%

Negative ROI is not always a failure — some investments are made for strategic reasons beyond immediate financial return (brand building, market entry, employee development).

Using ROI to Make Better Financial Decisions

  • Always calculate ROI before making significant investments
  • Compare the ROI of different options to allocate resources to the highest-return activities
  • Set minimum ROI thresholds for different types of investments (e.g., require 15% annualized ROI for stock investments)
  • Track actual ROI vs projected ROI to improve future estimates
  • Consider risk-adjusted ROI — a lower ROI with less risk may be preferable to a higher ROI with more risk
